The Buyer of Last Resort Just Arrived

Washington stopped arguing about which mine to back and started buying the market itself — a $12 billion stockpile, $14.8 billion in export-bank letters, and equity stakes in the companies it funds.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

In February 2026 the federal government stopped picking mines and started buying the market. Project Vault, announced on February 2, is a $12 billion public-private initiative to build a strategic stockpile of critical minerals, backed by a $10 billion direct loan from the Export-Import Bank and about $2 billion in private capital from companies including General Motors, Boeing, Google, and Stellantis. It is modeled on the Strategic Petroleum Reserve and designed to hold a 60-day emergency supply for American manufacturers. The same month, the Pentagon’s $110-a-kilogram price floor for neodymium-praseodymium started paying MP Materials when the market fell below it. Two different agencies, two different tools, one message: the buyer of last resort has arrived, and it is buying minerals.

The Export-Import Bank is the chequebook. Over the past year it has issued $14.8 billion in letters of interest for critical-minerals projects, including $455 million for rare-earth development and processing in the United States, $400 million for lithium extraction in Arkansas, $350 million for cobalt and nickel production in Australia, and $215 million for tin extraction in the United Kingdom and Australia. Its authorized portfolio includes the $10 billion Vault loan — more than double the largest financing in the bank’s history — and $1.3 billion for the Reko Diq copper and gold project in Pakistan. In August, at a roundtable at the State Department, the administration announced a further $3 billion package, the largest item a $1.4 billion conditional loan from the Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital to Sila Nanotechnologies to scale silicon-anode battery materials in Washington state. The Development Finance Corporation has mobilized more than $1 billion in mineral-related investments, including projects in Ukraine and Brazil. In total, the administration counts 160 minerals deals worth almost $40 billion signed or approved to date.

The Department of Energy’s Loan Programs Office is the other half of the machine, and it lends at a scale private credit cannot match. Its $2.26 billion loan to Lithium Americas for the Thacker Pass project in Nevada — designed to produce roughly 40,000 metric tons of battery-grade lithium carbonate a year in its first phase — produced its first $435 million drawdown in 2026, after the department restructured the loan to take a 5% equity stake in Lithium Americas and a 5% stake in the Thacker Pass joint venture with General Motors. The same office supports a $996 million loan for Ioneer’s Rhyolite Ridge project, a $475 million loan for Glencore battery recycling, and a $1.4 billion conditional commitment for EnergySource Minerals’ Project ATLiS, which extracts lithium hydroxide from geothermal brine. In August the department awarded $500 million in grants to seven companies, the largest $100 million each to Lilac Solutions for a direct lithium extraction facility on Utah’s Great Salt Lake, to the Jervois Group for the country’s only cobalt refinery in Idaho, and to Nth Cycle, backed by Trafigura, for a battery-metal scrap recycling facility. Westwater Resources took a $25 million EXIM investment for an Alabama graphite deposit, and Niron Magnetics secured a conditional commitment for a 20-year direct loan of up to $150 million for rare-earth-free iron nitride magnets in Sartell, Minnesota.

The honest version of the story is that a government buyer distorts the market it is trying to save. Price floors invite overbuilding: the higher the guaranteed price, the more capital rushes in, and the more supply arrives, the less the floor is needed. A stockpile that buys at the top can become the seller that caps the price when it stops buying. Equity stakes politicize exits — a government that owns 5% of a lithium company has opinions about when it sells. And the underlying commodity cycle has not repealed itself: Citi cut lithium price forecasts by up to 40% on slower battery demand, and lithium carbonate spot prices were still edging down in early November. The government can guarantee a buyer. It cannot guarantee a price.

But the financing logic has already changed, and that is what matters for anyone funding a project. A mine with a federal loan, a federal offtake, or a federal equity stake clears financing that a pure-market project cannot, because the lender’s risk is no longer the commodity price — it is the counterparty, and the counterparty is the United States. The investable universe is now defined by a list: the U.S. Geological Survey’s 2025 list of 60 critical minerals, covering lithium, nickel, cobalt, copper, uranium, graphite, and the rare earths. The question for a financier is no longer whether a project is economic at today’s spot price. It is whether the project is on the list, in a friendly jurisdiction, and in front of a buyer that has already said it will buy.
