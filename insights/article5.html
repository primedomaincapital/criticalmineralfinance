The Offtake Agreement: The Contract That Decides Whether a Mine Gets Financed

No bank funds a mine on a spot price. It funds it on a contract — and the offtake agreement is the document the whole financing hangs on.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

On February 10, 2026, an Australian lithium miner signed a piece of paper that did more for its balance sheet than any drill result that year. PLS Group, formerly Pilbara Minerals, agreed to supply 150,000 tonnes a year of spodumene concentrate to Canmax Technologies, a Chinese maker of lithium battery materials, for two years, with an option to extend for a third. The price would track the market — but never below US$1,000 a tonne for 6% concentrate, and with no ceiling at all. Canmax would pay US$100 million up front, interest-free, unsecured, repaid in product. PLS shares rose on the announcement. The reason was not the tonnage. It was the structure: a floor with no cap, cash before the first shipment, and a buyer whose signature a bank could underwrite.

That is what an offtake agreement is for. A mine is a hole in the ground that costs billions to dig and years to build, and no lender will fund it on the assumption that the commodity price cooperates. The offtake converts a price forecast into a contract: a named buyer, a named volume, a named pricing formula, and a named set of consequences if either side fails to perform. In project finance it is usually a condition precedent to the loan — the money does not move until the offtake is signed. The deposit, the plant, and the engineering study are the project. The offtake is the financing.

The PLS deal is the cleanest example of the modern form, and its terms are worth reading closely because each one answers a lender’s question. The floor of US$1,000 a tonne answers the downside: below that price, the buyer pays the floor anyway, so the miner’s revenue has a minimum and the lender’s debt service has a floor beneath it. The absence of a ceiling answers the upside: if lithium runs, the miner keeps the gain, which is what makes the equity investable. The US$100 million prepayment, interest-free and unsecured and repaid in spodumene rather than cash, answers the liquidity question: it is working capital that arrives before production, without covenants, and it demonstrates the buyer’s conviction in a way no press release can. The two-year term with a 12-month extension option answers the duration question — long enough to de-risk the loan, short enough that the miner is not locked into a price from another cycle.

Canmax did the same deal twice in three months. In December 2025, Liontown Resources signed a 150,000-tonne-a-year spodumene offtake with the same buyer, priced against market indices. The pattern is the point: when the buyer is a processor with furnaces to feed, offtakes are how it secures supply, and when the seller is a miner with a plant to fund, offtakes are how it secures the loan. PLS produced roughly 879,500 dry metric tonnes of spodumene in fiscal 2026, and in October 2026 its board approved the P2000 expansion — A$1.9 billion to lift capacity toward 2.0 million tonnes a year, with first production targeted for the September quarter of 2029. An expansion of that size does not get approved on a spot price. It gets approved on a book of contracts.

The offtake’s power cuts both ways, and the lithium cycle of the last two years shows the edge. Albemarle idled a train at its Kemerton hydroxide plant in Western Australia in February 2026, taking about 25,000 tonnes of annual hydroxide capacity offline, as prices stayed low. A miner with a floor keeps shipping into that market and keeps getting paid the floor. A miner without one idles a plant. The same logic applies to the buyer: a processor that signed a fixed-price offtake in a rising market watches its competitors buy cheaper on the spot, and a floor that looked generous at signing looks expensive when the market falls below it — which is precisely why Canmax paid a prepayment for the privilege, and why the floor came with no ceiling attached.

The structure is not limited to lithium. The United States government has become an offtake counterparty of last resort: the Pentagon’s $110-a-kilogram price floor for neodymium-praseodymium pays MP Materials when the market falls below it, and the Department of Energy’s $2.26 billion loan to Lithium Americas for Thacker Pass — designed to produce roughly 40,000 metric tons of battery-grade lithium carbonate a year in its first phase — came with the government taking a 5% equity stake in the company and in the joint venture with General Motors. The Export-Import Bank has issued $14.8 billion in letters of interest for critical-minerals projects. In each case the logic is the same as the PLS deal, with the counterparty changed: the lender’s risk is no longer the commodity price, it is the buyer, and the buyer is either a processor with furnaces to feed or a government with a stockpile to fill.

Beyond the price, the clauses that decide whether the contract survives a dispute are the ones nobody reads at signing. The quality specification — in the PLS deal, 6% spodumene concentrate, the SC6 grade — defines what the buyer must accept and what the seller must deliver, and a shipment that misses the grade is a shipment the buyer can reject. The volume flex sets how much the buyer can take above or below the contracted tonnage, and who bears the cost when the mine produces less than the contract promises. The delivery schedule, the shipping terms, and the force majeure clause decide what happens when a port closes, a rail line floods, or a government restricts exports — events that are not hypothetical in a market where the largest processor sits in a country that has used export controls as policy. And the default clause decides what the offtake is worth when one side fails: whether the injured party can terminate, claim damages, or draw on the prepayment. A contract that is silent on these points is not an offtake. It is a handshake with a signature, and the signature is worth less than the handshake when the dispute arrives.

The counterargument is the one every miner’s lawyer makes before signing, and it is real: an offtake can become the liability that outlives the cycle it was written for. A fixed-price contract signed at the top locks the miner into yesterday’s price while its costs rise with inflation. A take-or-pay clause obliges the buyer to pay whether or not it takes the material, which is protection until the buyer’s own business fails — and then the contract is a claim in someone else’s bankruptcy. A floor with no ceiling is elegant in a presentation and expensive in a negotiation, which is why buyers demand prepayments, volume flexibility, and extension options in return. The PLS structure works because both sides gave something up: the buyer paid cash up front and accepted the floor, and the seller accepted a two-year term and a single-buyer concentration on 150,000 tonnes a year.

That is the test for anyone reading an offtake, whether as a lender, an equity investor, or a counterparty. Four questions decide whether the contract does the job. First, does the pricing formula protect the downside without giving away the upside — a floor with no ceiling, or a fixed price that will look foolish in eighteen months? Second, is the buyer creditworthy enough that its signature is worth more than the paper it is written on — a processor with operating plants, or a trader with a letterhead? Third, does the volume match the mine’s production, or does the contract commit more tonnes than the plant can deliver, which turns a financing document into a default notice? And fourth, what happens when the contract ends — is there a renewal, an extension option, or a cliff where the revenue disappears the same quarter the loan needs refinancing? The PLS–Canmax deal answers all four, which is why it moved a share price. Most offtakes answer two. The ones that answer none are not financing documents. They are press releases with signatures.
