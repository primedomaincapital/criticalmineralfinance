How Much a Critical Minerals Project Costs to Build — and Where the Money Goes

The headline number is the easy part. The money goes to the plant, the power, the road, and the contingency line — and the contingency is where projects live or die.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The cost report lands on the project director’s desk every month, and every month the same line gets the first look. Not the total. The contingency. The total is a promise made in a feasibility study, in a year when steel was cheaper and the schedule was shorter. The contingency is what is left of that promise after reality has taken its share. On a critical minerals project, the contingency is where the project lives or dies, because the gap between the study and the ground is where the money actually goes.

Start with the scale. Lithium Americas’ Thacker Pass project in Nevada, designed to produce roughly 40,000 metric tons of battery-grade lithium carbonate a year in its first phase, carries a Department of Energy loan of $2.26 billion — and the loan is roughly the size of the build itself, which is why the department took a 5% equity stake in the company and a 5% stake in the Thacker Pass joint venture with General Motors when it restructured the loan in 2026. Ioneer’s Rhyolite Ridge project in Nevada is backed by a $996 million DOE loan. In uranium, NexGen Energy’s Rook I project in Saskatchewan carries a pre-production capital estimate of about C$2.2 billion, roughly US$1.55 billion, set in August 2024 and still unchanged in the company’s third-quarter 2026 report. Denison Mines’ Phoenix in-situ recovery project at Wheeler River was about 24% complete at September 30, 2026, after its first quarter of full-scale construction, with $161.1 million spent in the year to date and about $278.6 million expected for all of 2026. These are the numbers that define the asset class: a single mine costs one to two billion dollars to build, and the financing is the project.

Where does it go? The feasibility study breaks it into buckets, and the buckets are the same on every project, in roughly the same order of size. First, the processing plant — the crushers, the leach circuits, the refinery — which is usually the largest single line and the hardest to price, because it is the part that has never been built at this scale in this place before. Second, mine development: the shaft, the decline, the ground freezing, the fleet. At Rook I, ground freezing is planned for no later than March 2027 and shaft sinking for July 2027, with first ore delivery in the third quarter of 2030 — a four-year build in which the shaft is the critical path and the freezing plant is the long-lead item. Third, infrastructure: power, water, roads, camp, tailings. A mine in the Athabasca Basin or the Nevada desert does not plug into a grid; it builds one, or it pays for the line. Fourth, the indirects: engineering, procurement and construction management, the owner’s team, the permits, the insurance. Fifth, the contingency — and the working capital to run the plant until it pays for itself, which is a separate line that too many studies fold into the contingency and hope nobody notices.

The spending curve is not flat, and that matters for the financing. NexGen spent about $87 million on Rook I in the third quarter of 2026, roughly 65% more than the previous quarter, and chief executive Leigh Curyer confirmed on the company’s construction update call that spending would peak at about $420 million in 2027. The company is funded through the end of the third quarter of 2027, and its quarterly report states plainly that it “will continue to require additional financing” to finish the build — roughly US$1 billion still to be secured, through project debt, utility prepayments, and discussions that include BHP. The pattern is universal: the money goes out slowly while the ground is frozen and the shaft is sunk, then all at once when the plant arrives. A financing that is sized for the average quarter runs out in the peak quarter, and the peak quarter is where projects stall.

The financing mix is the second half of the cost, and it is where the government has changed the arithmetic. The Department of Energy’s loan portfolio for critical minerals now includes Thacker Pass at $2.26 billion, Rhyolite Ridge at $996 million, a $475 million loan for Glencore battery recycling, and a $1.4 billion conditional commitment for EnergySource Minerals’ Project ATLiS, which extracts lithium hydroxide from geothermal brine. The Export-Import Bank has issued $14.8 billion in letters of interest, and in August 2026 the administration announced a further $3 billion package, the largest item a $1.4 billion conditional loan from the Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital to Sila Nanotechnologies to scale silicon-anode battery materials. The government lends at a scale and a tenor private credit cannot match, and it lends against a project whose revenue is de-risked by an offtake or a price floor. That is the whole point of the public money: it is not a subsidy, it is a bridge over the part of the cost curve the private market will not fund alone.

The counterargument is the one every mining engineer makes and every financier ignores at their peril: the headline capital number understates the true cost of the project, and the understatement is systematic. The feasibility study prices the build. It does not price the ramp-up, the months when the plant runs at 40% while the debt service runs at 100%. It does not price the sustaining capital — the replacement fleet, the tailings dam lifts, the plant debottlenecking — that starts the day production starts. It does not price the interest during construction, which on a two-billion-dollar build at today’s rates is a nine-figure line of its own. And it does not price the contingency draw, which is not a reserve but a forecast: the study assumes a number, the ground assumes a bigger one, and the difference is funded by the equity that was supposed to be the return. NexGen’s own numbers tell the story from the other direction: Rook I is expected to produce at a cost per pound just under US$10, with an all-in cost of about US$14, against a uranium spot price quoted at about US$91 to US$92 a pound in the second week of November 2026. The margin is enormous. The margin is also the reason the market is willing to fund a four-year build with no revenue — and the reason the cost of that build is the only number that can take the margin away.

That is why the question to ask about any critical minerals project is not how much it costs. It is what is in the number, and what was left out of it. Is the contingency a percentage of direct costs, or a line that has already been drawn? Is working capital in the total, or in a footnote? Is the interest during construction capitalized, or is it a cash call in year three? Does the study assume the plant ramps in six months, or in eighteen, and who pays the debt service in the gap? The projects that get built are not the ones with the lowest headline number. They are the ones whose number survived contact with the ground — and whose financing was sized for the peak quarter, not the average one.
