The Price Floor, Tested: What Happens When the Government’s Guarantee Meets the Market

A price floor is a promise the market will test. The first test arrived within months — and the answer says as much about the guarantee as about the metal.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

In July 2025 the Pentagon did something no government had done for a rare earths company: it bought the downside. The deal with MP Materials set a price floor of $110 a kilogram for neodymium-praseodymium, the magnet metals at the center of the electric vehicle and defense supply chains, and the Pentagon agreed to pay the difference whenever the market fell below it — alongside a $400 million equity investment that made the government the company’s largest shareholder. The floor was the point. A mine that cannot survive a price collapse cannot get financed, and a floor that pays when the price collapses is the cheapest way to make it financeable. The market did not wait long to test it.

The test arrived in the form of the commodity cycle itself. Lithium, the metal that set the template for the whole critical minerals boom, spent 2026 falling: Citi cut its lithium price forecasts by up to 40% on slower battery demand, and lithium carbonate spot prices were still edging down in early November. Albemarle idled a train at its Kemerton hydroxide plant in Western Australia in February 2026, taking about 25,000 tonnes of annual capacity offline. The rare earths market is smaller and less transparent than lithium, but the mechanism is identical: when the price falls below the cost of the marginal producer, production stops, and the floor is what decides whether it stops at a mine in California or a mine in China. The Pentagon’s floor does not set the price. It sets the minimum revenue of the one producer it covers, and in doing so it changes the financing of every producer that does not have one.

The floor is one instrument in a toolkit that has grown very large, very fast. Project Vault, announced on February 2, 2026, is a $12 billion public-private initiative to build a strategic stockpile of critical minerals, backed by a $10 billion direct loan from the Export-Import Bank and about $2 billion in private capital from companies including General Motors, Boeing, Google, and Stellantis, designed to hold a 60-day emergency supply for American manufacturers. The Export-Import Bank has issued $14.8 billion in letters of interest for critical-minerals projects. The Department of Energy’s Loan Programs Office has lent $2.26 billion to Lithium Americas for Thacker Pass — designed to produce roughly 40,000 metric tons of battery-grade lithium carbonate a year in its first phase — and taken a 5% equity stake in the company and in the joint venture with General Motors. In August 2026 the administration announced a further $3 billion package, the largest item a $1.4 billion conditional loan from the Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital to Sila Nanotechnologies, and the Department of Energy awarded $500 million in grants to seven companies. The administration counts 160 minerals deals worth almost $40 billion signed or approved to date. The floor is the most visible instrument. It is not the only one, and it is not the largest.

What the floor actually does, mechanically, is convert a commodity risk into a counterparty risk. A lender funding MP Materials no longer has to underwrite the neodymium-praseodymium price. It has to underwrite the Pentagon — its willingness to pay, its budget, its politics, and the durability of a deal struck by one administration and inherited by the next. That is a different risk, and for most lenders it is a smaller one, which is why the floor works as a financing instrument. But it is not no risk. A floor is a contract with a government, and governments change their minds. The stockpile that buys at the top can become the seller that caps the price when it stops buying. The equity stake that de-risks the financing politicizes the exit: a government that owns a large share of a company has opinions about when it sells, and to whom.

Mechanically, a floor is simpler than it sounds, and the simplicity is part of its appeal to a lender. The contract names a reference price — an index, a published assessment, or a basket of transactions — and a settlement period, usually monthly or quarterly. When the reference price falls below the floor, the guarantor pays the difference on the volume produced in that period. When the reference price is above the floor, nothing is paid and the producer sells at the market. The payment is a top-up, not a purchase: the government does not take delivery of the metal, it takes delivery of the risk, and the metal goes to the buyer who wanted it in the first place. The precedent is older than the rare earths debate. The United States has run price supports for agricultural commodities for nearly a century, and the Strategic Petroleum Reserve is a buyer of last resort for oil, filling when prices are low and releasing when they spike. The floor applies the same logic to a metal the defense industrial base cannot do without, and the mechanics are the same: the government pays when the market will not, and stops paying when it will.

The second-order effect is the one the market is still working through: a floor invites overbuilding. The higher the guaranteed price, the more capital rushes in, and the more supply arrives, the less the floor is needed — until the floor itself becomes the marginal price, and the market stops discovering anything at all. The lithium experience is the warning: a wave of capacity financed on high prices arrived just as demand slowed, and the price fell through every forecast. A floor does not prevent that cycle. It transfers the cost of it from the miner to the taxpayer, and it does so at exactly the moment when the taxpayer is least inclined to notice, which is the moment the price is falling and the floor is paying.

The counterargument is the one the Pentagon made when it signed the deal, and it is not a small one: the alternative to a floor is not a free market, it is a market dominated by a single supplier that has already shown it will use that dominance. China mines about 60% of the magnet rare earths, refines 91% of them, and makes 94% of the magnets. A price floor for the one American producer is not a distortion of a competitive market. It is a counterweight to a market that is not competitive in the first place. The floor does not have to be efficient. It has to be sufficient — sufficient to keep a domestic producer alive through the part of the cycle when the dominant supplier can afford to lose money and the challenger cannot.

That is the honest frame for the test, and it is the frame the next few years will judge. The floor will be tested again, in lithium and in rare earths and in the next metal the stockpile buys, and each test will answer the same three questions. Does the floor hold when the price falls — does the government actually pay, on time, in full, without renegotiation? Does the floor change the financing — do lenders fund projects they would not have funded without it, at terms that reflect the guarantee rather than the commodity? And does the floor outlive the politics that created it — does the next budget, the next administration, the next Congress keep a promise made to a mine in California? The first test arrived within months. The answers will take a cycle to arrive, and the cycle is the thing the floor was built to survive.
