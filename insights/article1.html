Sixty Names on a List

The U.S. critical minerals list grew from 50 to 60 minerals in November 2025. The designation matters — and it does less than most people assume.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 6, 2026 · 7 min read

On November 7, 2025, the U.S. Geological Survey published a list. Sixty minerals long. Ten longer than the one before it. Copper was on it now, and uranium, and silver — commodities most people would have called ordinary the day before. Nothing in the ground had changed overnight. What changed was the paperwork: the federal government had formally decided that these sixty materials were essential to the economy or to national security, and that their supply could not be taken for granted.

The list is the second update of its kind. It began with a 2017 executive order that set the United States on a path to measuring its mineral vulnerabilities, and the Energy Act of 2020 now requires the Department of the Interior to review and update it every three years. The 2022 list held 50 minerals. The final 2025 list keeps all 50 and adds ten: boron, copper, lead, metallurgical coal, phosphate, potash, rhenium, silicon, silver, and uranium. Fifteen of the sixty are rare earth elements.

The new list was built differently from the old one. Instead of ranking minerals by how much the country imports, the Geological Survey modeled more than 1,200 trade-disruption scenarios across 84 mineral commodities, measuring what a cutoff would cost 402 individual industries and the economy as a whole. The draft list, published in August 2025, held 54 commodities. The final list grew to 60. The ten minerals judged most exposed to disruption, in descending order of probability-weighted economic impact, were samarium, rhodium, lutetium, terbium, dysprosium, gallium, germanium, gadolinium, tungsten, and niobium — a roll call dominated by elements most Americans have never heard of, and which almost no American mine produces at scale.

The dependence the list describes is stark. The United States relies on foreign suppliers — often China and Russia — for more than half of its supply of at least 46 of the 60 minerals on the list, according to the Geological Survey's director. That single sentence is the commercial case for the entire sector: a country that cannot supply itself with the materials its own economy runs on will pay, one way or another, to change that. The list is the map of where the money has to go.

Here is the part the headlines usually skip. Getting on the list does not, by itself, build a mine. The designation guides federal investment, permitting decisions, stockpiles, tax incentives, and processing support — but the most concrete tax incentive in the system has not caught up with the new list at all. The Section 45X advanced manufacturing production credit remains available through 2030, but only for the 50 minerals on the 2022 list. As of the final list's publication, no effort was underway to amend the tax code so the credit covers the ten new additions. Copper, the metal every grid and every electric vehicle needs in bulk, is now officially critical and still outside the credit that was written for critical minerals.

That gap is the honest test of what the list is worth. A designation that steers permitting and federal attention is real value — a mine that reaches production two years sooner because its permits moved faster is a mine that earns two more years of revenue. But a designation that does not yet carry the tax treatment, the offtake, or the financing is a signal, not a subsidy. The ten new minerals sit in that gap today, and the financiers who understand the difference between the two will price deals better than the ones who treat the list as a guarantee.

The implication for anyone financing this sector is to read the list the way a lender reads a credit rating: as a statement about risk, not a promise of return. Ask which minerals on the list have real federal money behind them, which have only the label, and which have neither. The sixty names are the starting point of the analysis. They are not the end of it.
